# How working with us goes

Small companies usually describe their process in a way that could describe any
company. This is the specific version, including the parts that are inconvenient
for us to say.

[What we build](/services/what-we-build/) is the capability side.
[{{MAIL}}](mailto:{{MAIL}}) is how to start.

## The first email

Write to [{{MAIL}}](mailto:{{MAIL}}). There is no form, no discovery-call
scheduler, and nobody in front of it.

**What is useful in a first email:** what the software has to do, who it is for,
and what is forcing the timing — a launch, a contract, a platform deadline, a thing
that is currently done by hand. A paragraph is plenty. A specification is welcome
and not expected.

**What happens next:** you get a real answer, usually within a few days, from
someone who has read it. That answer is either "yes, and here is what we would want
to know next", or "no, and here is why, and here is the shape of company that does
this well."

## We tell you no, quickly

More often than a company trying to fill a pipeline would admit to.

Sending you elsewhere costs us one email. Taking on work that is the wrong fit
costs both of us months and ends with a project neither party wants to talk about.
The specific cases where we will say no are
[listed on the services page](/services/#when-we-are-the-wrong-choice) rather than
left for you to discover in week six.

## We agree what "done" means before starting

Not a fifty-page specification that nobody reads after signature. A short,
specific, written statement of what the software will do — behaviour, and the
conditions under which each piece is finished.

The point of writing it down is not ceremony. It is that **"done" should be a fact
rather than an opinion.** Without that, the end of a project is a negotiation
between two tired people, and the person with more stamina wins rather than the
person who is right.

If we cannot write that statement for your project yet, that is worth knowing
before money changes hands — and it usually means the next piece of work is a short
paid exploration rather than a build.

## You talk to the people building it

There is no account manager, no delivery lead and no weekly status call whose
purpose is to have happened.

The trade is real and you should weigh it. **What you get:** the person answering
your question is the person who wrote the code, so the answer is the actual answer
and not a relayed one, and a decision does not have to travel through anybody. **What
you give up:** the reassurance apparatus. No dashboard with a green light on it, no
account team, and no illusion that the work continues at full speed while somebody
is ill.

## What you hear from us while it is happening

**Short, specific, and including the bad news.** What is finished, what is not,
what changed, what we got wrong.

That last one is the load-bearing part and it is the same standard the rest of this
site is held to. This website publishes
[which of our own products are not built](/status/),
[which accessibility work has not been done](/accessibility/), and
[the fact that nothing we own has ever been security-audited](/security/). We are
not going to hold our own shop window to that standard and then send you a status
report with the difficult column removed.

**And if a date is going to slip, you hear it when we know**, not on the date. A
schedule is a forecast; the useful thing about a forecast is how early it updates.

## Estimates, honestly

**We will estimate.** We will tell you what we think something takes and what the
estimate is sensitive to.

**We will not quote a fixed price for a fixed scope that nobody has specified.**
Not caution — arithmetic. The only way to hit a number set before the problem is
understood is to cut something, and the thing that gets cut is the part you cared
about, because it is always the part that turned out to be hard.

**We will not pad an estimate to protect ourselves and call it a buffer.** Where
something is genuinely uncertain we would rather name the uncertainty and agree how
to reduce it cheaply — usually by building the risky part first.

## We would rather ship a shorter list that works

A recurring decision in every project: four features that mostly work, or two that
work.

We will argue for two, every time, and we will lose that argument sometimes because
it is your product. What we will not do is agree to four and then quietly deliver
four half-features and a status report that says green.

## Ownership, and what happens if we stop

**You own what we build for you** — the code, the data, the accounts it runs in.
That is the default and we are not interested in arrangements where it is not.

**We will not build you something only we can maintain.** Any project we hand over
should be legible to whoever comes next: written down, tested, and deployable by
someone who was not in the room. The
[approach to dependencies](/services/what-we-build/#front-ends-that-still-work-in-five-years)
is part of the same instinct — a build that needs four hundred packages and a
specific machine is a build you cannot take anywhere.

That matters more than usual with a company this size, and we would rather you
raise it than worry about it.

## What we will not do in your product

The same commitments our own products are held to. They do not come off for a fee.

**No dark patterns.** No manufactured streaks, no infinite scroll where a list
would do, no notification bait, nothing designed to make leaving hard.

**No advertising surveillance we would not accept ourselves.** We will not bolt a
tracking SDK into your product and then help write a privacy policy that talks
around it.

**No claim we cannot support** — not in the software, not in the store listing, not
in the privacy policy. If a document has to say something is true, it has to be
true, and where a decision has not been made the document records the silence
rather than filling it in.

That third one is a genuine constraint on what we will put our name to, and it is
better to read it here than to discover it during a submission.
